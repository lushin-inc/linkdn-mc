Section 01 · 3 min

TeamLink Extend

1 TeamLink Extend pools your whole company's network

  • Enterprise only: TeamLink Extend is available only on the enterprise (corporate) version of Sales Navigator.
  • What it is: the pooled networks of everyone at your company who opted in to share their network with the sales team — finance, product, engineering, people you've never met.
  • Why it's big: it leverages connections from people's personal lives — neighbors, in-laws, college roommates — to show who at your company already knows your top prospects.
  • Two different numbers: your common-connections count is your own network; the TeamLink Extend number in the center of the profile draws on every opted-in colleague's network, so it will be different.

2 Read a prospect's profile for warm paths

Open the prospect's profile in Navigator

Scroll to the relationship section.

Check common connections

Example: 15 connections in common means you and the target are both first-degree connected to the same 15 people — you can probably pull strings to get on their radar.

Check the TeamLink Extend number

See who in your company — anyone opted in, in any department — is connected to this prospect.

Pick the strongest path and ask for the intro

The ask script is on the Ask for an Intro Internally page.

Key

TeamLink Extend is your first stop and common connections your second stop for opening a door to any prospect — skip them and you're missing the mark.

Uncovering Connections

1 Map who you know inside a target account

  • The problem: a big account page shows tens of thousands of employees (DuPont example: 36,000) — you're not going to wade through that.
  • The shortcut: the Decision Makers link on the account page is a pre-populated saved search built by LinkedIn — anyone with a C-suite, Director, or VP title (about 2,000 results in the example).
  • The question you're answering: do I know anybody in the decision-making tree that would be of help to me?

2 Run the check

Go to the company's account page in Navigator

Note the shortcut links at the top, including the all-employees count.

Click Decision Makers

LinkedIn's pre-built saved search returns everyone with a C-suite, Director, or VP title.

Filter Relationship to 1st-degree connections

On the left-hand side, click through to see: do I know anyone that works here?

If no decision makers hit, widen to all employees

Go back to the full employee list, rinse and repeat — do I know anybody here at all?

Key

The moment you get a new account or a new book of business, map it: who you know, and who knows who, that can be of help to you.

Section 03 · 3 min

Ask for an Intro Internally

1 Asking a colleague you've never met

  • The situation: TeamLink Extend surfaces colleagues you've never met — and you're asking them to put a personal relationship on the line for you.
  • Rule one — context is key: introduce yourself, explain what you do, and give as much information as possible.
  • Rule two — use any medium: email, chat, whatever works; be polite either way.
  • The failure mode: "Sam, it's Bob. Can you introduce me to this really high level CRO?" — the recipient thinks: who are you, Bob? Why should I put my relationship on the line to help you?
  • Make it easy to want to introduce you: the whole message is built to lower the cost of saying yes.

2 The ask, word for word

Internal intro ask — replace the bracketed variables

Betsy, [we've] yet to be properly introduced, but I'm Sam McKenna and I'm an AE on the sales team here at [company I work for]. I'm trying to open some doors at LinkedIn and I see that you're connected to [First Name Last Name — hyperlink their LinkedIn profile]. I've been trying to get a meeting with her for a few months given that LinkedIn is an existing client of ours and that we're trying to close some additional business. Do you happen to know her well enough to see if she would be up for an introduction? I'm happy to ghostwrite the email for you or give you further context if you'd prefer.

3 Why each piece works

  • Hyperlink the target's profile: so the colleague can click the name and go "yeah, that's right, I know this person" instead of "who is this? I don't even know this person."
  • "Do you happen to know her well enough...?": never assume the relationship is real — we all have plenty of LinkedIn connections we don't actually know.
  • Offer to ghostwrite the email: people fielding lots of requests don't want to hand-write intros every time; the offer makes it easy to say "yes, I'm happy to help."
  • Offer further context: sometimes the ask alone isn't enough for someone to risk their reputation — being proactive here makes you stand out and makes them want to help.

Executive Power Hours

1 One hour mining an executive's personal network

  • When to run it: you have target accounts (like DuPont) where nobody on the team knows anyone.
  • The play: get one of your executives — the CRO, for example — on the phone with your five- or six-person sales team for one hour.
  • Why it works: the exec runs the same connection-mining exercise on their own personal LinkedIn, which the team otherwise has zero visibility into.
  • The framing: Second or Third Degrees of Kevin Bacon — but with your executive team.

2 How the hour runs

Book one hour with an executive and the sales team

Bring the list of target accounts.

First-degree check per account

"Do you have any first degree connections at DuPont, Mr. CRO?"

Second-degree check

If not, the exec pulls the same search and goes to second-degree connections — do they know anybody that knows somebody at the account?

Let the exec annotate the relationships

"I know this person really well" — "this person owes me a favor." Only they can grade the strength of their own connections.

Match targets to paths

Find the people you most want in front of and the person the exec knows who can get you there.

  • The principle: when you're cracking open accounts or expanding within existing clients, the absolute fastest path in is through the relationships you have.
  • Pick the right champion: someone who knows your product and knows you — the example is Robin, a client of over a decade, who will be willing to recommend you.
  • The goal: find everyone Robin knows that you want to get in front of, then ask her to broker introductions.

2 Route A — cross-reference her connections against your saved leads and accounts

Search the champion's name from the Navigator homepage

Open her profile.

Click her 500+ connections

This opens her full first-degree connection list. There's sometimes a cap on what LinkedIn will show — the demo surfaced 997 of Robin's connections.

Find Past Lead and Account Activity on the left and click the plus sign

The filters on the left all search within her first-degree connections.

Choose Search within saved accounts

Anyone she knows who is also in your saved individuals and saved accounts surfaces. Demo: Robin knows 12 individuals Sam wants to get in front of.

Take the list back to your champion

Vet each name with the questions below, then ask for the introduction.

Champion vetting questions — ask before requesting the intro

Hey, how do you know Darrell? How do you know Andrew? Do you know them well? Do you have good experience with them? Are these people that you wouldn't mind writing an introduction for me to open up that account, open up a conversation, and hopefully get a meeting booked with them?

3 Route B — no saved leads or accounts yet

X out of the saved-accounts filter

Add Geography within her connections

  • Title search is smart: searching Chief Marketing Officer also matches Marketing Director; Director of Business Development also matches Director, BD — you don't have to type every variation in.
  • Who to run this on: your best clients, your best friends, your law school roommate — anyone whose connections you can capitalize on to open relationships.

Profile Views — The Navigator Advantage

1 Visibility rules — free LinkedIn vs Navigator

5
last viewers visible on free LinkedIn
90
days of viewers visible with Navigator

Free LinkedIn.com

  • Last 5 only: if 10 people look at your profile today, you can only see the last 5.
  • Reciprocity applies: whatever privacy you give is what you get back.
  • Fully visible: viewers see your name and title, and you see theirs.
  • Fully anonymous: you see no one who looks at your profile.

With Sales Navigator

  • No 5-viewer cap: you see everyone who viewed your profile in the last 90 days.
  • One-way privacy: you can browse completely private and still see everybody who views you.
  • One exception: viewers who set themselves to anonymous stay hidden.
Key

Navigator removes both limitations at once — full 90-day viewer history plus the ability to stay private while seeing everyone who looks at you.

2 The four views to check

  • All profile views: scroll through everyone; watch the trend line for spikes tied to posts that performed well.
  • Interesting views: LinkedIn aggregates the viewers who match who you care about — sales leaders, sales enablement leaders, chief revenue officers, heads of marketing.
  • Aggregations: viewers grouped by company and cohort, so you can see clusters from one organization or title.
  • Found you via LinkedIn search: people physically typing your name into the search bar — they did not find you organically; someone said your name, they read about you, or they heard you on a podcast.
  • Repeat visitors: note who keeps coming back — that pattern matters.

3 The outreach campaign on your viewers

  • Who to contact: viewers within your target market — an SVP of Sales or a CRO at a company in your ICP gets proactive outreach.
  • Name the overlap: specifically state who you have in common — pull one or two names out.
  • Stay relaxed: a less salesy note motivates the person to reply with "actually, you can be of help."
Viewer outreach — connection request to a 2nd/3rd-degree viewer

Hi, saw you pop by my profile. Would love to connect. See we have some common connections. Let me know if I can ever be of help.

Viewer outreach — direct message to a 1st-degree viewer, naming shared connections

Saw you happen to look at my profile. Would love to get in touch. By the way, I see we share some common connections in [specific person] and [specific person]. If I can ever do anything to be of help to you, let me know.

Do this

  • Relaxed, helpful tone — "let me know if I can ever be of help"
  • Pull one or two shared-connection names into the note
  • Let them tell you what they want to talk about

Not this

  • "I need to schedule a 30-minute call"
  • Aggressive asks irritate the person
  • Pushiness reads as salesy and desperate

Saving Leads — Secret Sleuthing

1 What saving a lead does

  • Works on anyone: 1st degree, 2nd degree, 3rd degree — any member of LinkedIn can be saved from their profile.
  • Completely invisible: no matter what your privacy settings are, the person will not know they have been saved.
  • Feeds your alerts: notifications about the person start appearing in your Navigator feed.
  • Auto-follows their company: saving a person saves their company as an account too — no separate step needed.
Key

Saving is silent surveillance for sellers — you build a watchlist of buyers, customers, and targets, and none of them ever knows.

2 How to save someone

Open the person's profile

Works whether you are connected or not.

Hover the Save button and hit Save

The button sits at the top of the profile.

Choose the list

Drop them into your saved leads or a specific lead list. They are now part of your environment — people you want to sell to, existing customers, or people to keep tabs on.

3 Why save each connection degree

1st-degree connections

  • Never miss their updates: being connected does not mean you see what they post — saving does.
  • Key buyers in your book: saving gives you information on them and their company in one move.

2nd/3rd-degree connections

  • Too early to connect: keep tabs on buyers where a connection request is not yet appropriate.
  • Intel gathering: watch people you have never met without them knowing.

The Navigator Inbox

1 The separate-inbox gotcha

  • Two inboxes: the Sales Navigator inbox is separate from your LinkedIn.com inbox.
  • The trap: send an InMail from Navigator and get a reply — you will not see it on LinkedIn.com. The reply lives only in the Navigator inbox.
  • The habit: if you send from Navigator, check the Navigator inbox for replies.

2 Why it works that way — seller mechanics

  • InMail sends an email: the message goes straight to the recipient's email, which lifts response rates.
  • Automatic reminder: if they do not read it, a follow-up nudge goes out in a few days — "your message from Sam is still awaiting your reply."
  • No Navigator required: the recipient does not need Sales Navigator to receive an InMail — it is just your sending mechanism.
  • Push notifications: a message sent from Navigator to a 1st-degree connection triggers a push notification on their phone — basically like texting them.

3 When to use it — and when to skip it

  • InMail does perform: it gets a better response rate than email.
  • But it is not the point of Navigator: this is not one of the end-all-be-all features.
  • Use it in a pinch: when you want the push notification or the automatic reminder working for you.
  • Otherwise: leave it to the side and keep messaging people through your LinkedIn inbox.

Recommended Leads

1 How recommended leads are generated

  • Where they appear: the Recommended Leads module on any profile shows people who might be of interest around that buyer.
  • The mechanism: when a buyer takes meetings, reps look up everyone on the invite — typing one name, then the next.
  • The grouping: LinkedIn registers that these people were searched for together and infers they are on the same team.
  • The threads build: as more reps repeat the pattern, the algorithm concludes these people are part of the same influencing or decision-making team.
  • The result: recommended leads are algorithmic research telling you who is important to your buyer.

2 The play

  • Save the buyer — and everyone recommended around them: not just the person you met.
  • What you gain: visibility into what the people around your core decision-making team are posting and sharing.
  • Bonus on live deals: knowing what is going on in their sphere makes you look smarter on every call.

3 The monster-deal mechanics
