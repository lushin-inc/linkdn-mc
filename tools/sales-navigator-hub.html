1 Start from closed business, not from a filter

Before you touch a search filter, pull the list of everyone Lushin has ever closed, lost, or quoted. That list is the raw material for six of the nine searches later — it is the single highest-value input to this whole system, and it takes an afternoon.

Closed won, still active

Every current client contact. These become your referral sources and your alumni-search seed list. Browning Day, Keramida, Hubexo, Home Nation, Next Modular, PartnerCentric, Visterra, Rediem, Safety Net.

Closed won, since churned or gone quiet

Why did it end? If it ended for a reason that had nothing to do with the work — budget, a champion left, a reorg — that contact is a live prospect the moment they surface somewhere new.

Closed lost and no-decision, last 18 months

Pull these by close date. Prioritize the oldest window first — 12 to 18 months back — because that is where the buyer is most likely to have changed jobs since. A buyer who blocked you at their old company is a different buyer at their new one.

Quoted but never closed

Anyone who saw a proposal and went dark. They know what you do and roughly what it costs. That is most of the education already done.

Key

Every one of those people is a node. The searches in section 2 of this hub take those names and turn them into hundreds of warm paths you currently cannot see.

2 Tier the accounts

Ten at a time. Not forty, not two hundred. Build the process around the first ten, then move to the next ten.

TierWhat goes hereHow many
Tier 1Logos you would most want on the Lushin list. Right size, right industry, and you can name a reason they should care.10–25
Tier 2Fits the profile, no particular urgency or angle yet.50–100
Tier 3In the market but not worth active work. Lives in a saved search and an alert, nothing more.Unlimited
ClientsCurrent engagements. Tracked for expansion and for champion job changes.~14
AlumniFormer clients and closed-lost accounts. Tracked for reactivation.As many as you have

Work one industry at a time rather than jumping between them. A week on AEC firms, a week on environmental consulting, a week on modular and manufacturing. You keep the same language in your head all week and it costs you far less energy.

3 Buyer titles

Sales Navigator's title filter expands automatically — enter VP of Sales and it also returns Vice President, Sales and Sales Vice President. You do not need to enumerate every variant.

Economic buyer

President, Owner, CEO, Managing Partner, Principal. At a 20–150 person firm this is usually the person who signs.

Sales leader

VP of Sales, Director of Sales, Sales Manager, Chief Revenue Officer, National Sales Manager.

Adjacent

Director of Business Development, VP of Operations, Chief Growth Officer, Head of Sales Enablement.

Not your buyer

Marketing titles, HR and L&D at small firms, individual reps. Exclude these in your saved search base.

  • Geography: Indiana, plus Chicago, Cincinnati, Louisville and Columbus metros. Add national only where an existing client gives you a warm path in.
  • Headcount: 20–250. Below 20 there is rarely a sales team to train; above 250 you are competing with enterprise L&D procurement.
  • Industries: Architecture & engineering, construction and building products, environmental and sustainability consulting, commercial real estate, manufacturing, landscaping, B2B software.
  • The tell that matters most: a firm that has recently hired its first salesperson, promoted someone into sales from a technical role, or posted a sales opening. Safety Net is exactly that shape — owner sold everything himself for 28 years, just moved someone into sales.
  • Sam McKenna (SamSales) — the warm-path system. Low volume, high title, referral-led. Sales Navigator is the engine: searches that mine relationships you already have. Everything in Lists & Searches and most of Warm paths is hers.
  • Ty Frankel (LinkedIn Client Lab) — a cold-volume DM system. High request volume, conversation held entirely in the DMs. He barely uses Sales Navigator. Everything in DM fundamentals, The micro moves, Asking for the meeting and Follow-up is his.
  • Where they disagree, this hub says so on the page and picks one for your deal size. There are four of those: note vs. blank connection requests, request volume, InMail, and how fast you pitch.

1 Work backwards from the number, not forwards from activity

Both courses hand out daily activity numbers. Ignore both until you have done this, because their numbers are calibrated to their deal sizes, not yours. One of them sells a $5,000 coaching program at a 20% close rate. You do not.

Start with the revenue you want to self-source this year

Not total production. The portion you intend to bring in yourself.

Divide by average engagement value

Use what a first-year Lushin engagement actually bills, not what the biggest one billed.

That is your deals needed

Round up.

Divide by your close rate from first meeting

Use your real number. If you don't know it, use 20% — it is the conservative industry figure and it is better to plan on a bar you will beat.

1 The profile is a filter, not a brochure

Two moments decide whether this system works, and your profile drives both. When someone gets your connection request, most of them click your profile before deciding. When someone gets your DM, most of them click your profile before replying. A weak profile costs you at both gates, and no amount of message craft recovers it.

2 What to change

Headline

Who you work with, what changes for them, and one number. Not your job title. "I help owners and sales leaders at 20–250 person firms build a sales process that doesn't depend on them" beats "Sales Coach & Trainer at Lushin."

Photo and banner

You, clean background, matched to the banner. The banner is prime real estate — put the outcome or the proof there, not a stock skyline.

Custom button

Set it to your booking link. It is one click from every profile view you get, and profile views are one of your warmest prospect sources.

About section

How you got here, then proof, then what you do, then a raw booking link. LinkedIn does not hyperlink text in About, so paste the URL plainly.

Featured

Four or five pieces of proof. Client results, a session recording, a portal you built for a client. Numbers and specifics, nothing abstract.

Experience

Prune to what builds trust with an Indiana firm owner. Anything that shows you have run something, kept. Anything irrelevant, gone.

Skills

Only the top two display. Pick the two that match what you sell and get them endorsed heavily.

Recommendations

Two, from clients whose names carry weight locally, with a number in them.

3 The formatting rule that actually matters

Vary your line lengths and break every block. Nobody reads a paragraph on LinkedIn. Short line, longer line, short line. Numbers on their own lines. This applies to your About, your Experience entries, and every post.

3 InMail

Sam is blunt that InMail's day has passed — buyers read it as paid spam and delete it. She is right for cold outreach. There are two narrow cases where it still earns its place:

Worth it

You have no other channel and the title justifies a credit. InMail triggers an email to them and a reminder from LinkedIn if they don't open it — that reminder is the actual value.

Not worth it

Anywhere you could send a connection request with a note instead, or where you already have their email. Which is most of the time.

  • InMail replies land in the Sales Navigator inbox, not your LinkedIn.com inbox. If you send an InMail and never check Navigator's inbox, you will miss the reply.
  • Messages you send from Navigator to a first-degree connection push to their phone as a notification, which is closer to a text than an email.
  • Practical rule: send your normal DMs from LinkedIn.com so everything lives in one thread. Use Navigator's inbox only when you have actually spent an InMail credit.

1 Four things, two jobs

These four names cause more confusion than anything else in the tool. They are two objects and two containers, and the whole point of them is to switch on alerts.

What it isWhat it actually does
Saved Lead
a person		Hitting Save on someone's profile. They are never notified, at any connection degree. From that moment Navigator watches them for you: job changes, posts, news mentions, and whether they view your profile.
Lead List
a folder of people		A named container for saved leads. The list view is the useful part — it shows you at a glance how many changed jobs, how many posted in the last 30 days, and how many follow the Lushin company page. That header row is where the work comes from.
Saved Account
a company		Same idea for a company. Switches on account growth, hiring, news mentions, company posts, new decision makers, and risk signals.
Account List
a folder of companies		A named container for saved accounts. This is what your tiering lives in, and it is the thing you drop into the Current company filter in one click instead of typing 40 company names.

2 The mechanic that saves you the most work

Key

Saving a person automatically saves their company as an account. You do not have to save both. Build your lead lists and your account list populates itself.

This runs one direction only. Saving a company does not save the people. So the order is: find the person, save the person, and the account comes along.

3 Why you save a first-degree connection

It looks pointless — you are already connected, you already see them. Two reasons it is not:

  • You do not actually see them. The LinkedIn feed shows you a fraction of what your connections post. A saved lead's posts surface in Navigator's alerts feed every time.
  • You want the company too. Saving Tabitha at Browning Day means you now get Browning Day's headcount growth, news mentions and new-decision-maker alerts, for free, without ever saving the account.
  • Every contact on a closed-won deal. When they move, that is a warm opportunity at a new logo and a hole to fill at the old one.
  • Every contact on a closed-lost deal. Same, and often better — the person who couldn't get it approved at their old firm can approve it at their new one.
  • Every champion at a current client. Nick at Keramida, Tabitha at Browning Day, Chuck at Bulwark. Their moves are your best leads and you want to know the day it happens.
  • Every blocker. Save them and label them. The day a blocker leaves is the day the account reopens.
  • Anyone who views your profile and fits. Covered in Outreach.

2 Seven lead lists

People. Two kinds: stage lists, where a person sits in exactly one at a time and moves forward; and standing lists, where they live permanently.

Stage lists — a lead is in exactly one

L1 — Vetting

You have found them and commented on one post. Not yet earned a connection request. Cap this at 100. Notes read: DF commented 09/09.

L2 — Approved

Two or three comments in, connection request sent or accepted. This is where DMs happen.

L3 — Asked

You have made the direct ask for a meeting. Note the date. This is the list you sweep every Friday for follow-ups.

L4 — Parked

Asked, no reply after the follow-up sequence, or they said not now. Revisit at 90–120 days. This list should be your biggest and it should not bother you.

Standing lists — a lead lives here permanently

S1 — Top 50

The come-hell-or-high-water names. Your Monday block works this list and nothing else. Cap it at roughly 50 so the rotation stays under five weeks.

S2 — Job Hoppers

Every contact attached to a closed deal, won or lost. You are not working this list — you are waiting for it to fire a job-change alert.

S3 — Champions

People who would vouch for you. Worked twice a year for referral asks, and watched for moves.

Key

A lead in a stage list moves; it never gets copied. If Nick is in L2 and you ask him for a meeting, he leaves L2 and enters L3. One person, one stage, always. This is the single rule that keeps the system from rotting.

3 Naming

  • Prefix everything. L2 — Approved, not Approved. The prefix forces sort order and stops you dropping someone in the wrong list.
  • Never two lists whose names look alike at a glance. Sam's own warning: she names hers Vetting and Vetted and Approved and still nearly mis-files people. Prefixes solve it.
  • Split by persona once a list passes ~100. S1 — Top 50 AEC and S1 — Top 50 Manufacturing beats one list of 200 you never open.

These are Sam's searches, translated to your book. Each one asks Navigator a question you could not answer any other way. Build them in this order — the early ones need nothing but your account list, the later ones need the closed-deal work from Setup.

2 The searches

  • Current company: one Tier 1 account, or drop in your A1 account list.
  • Connection: 1st degree.
  • Then narrow by geography and title if the list is long.

What you are looking for: not the buyer. Anyone you know well enough to ask a favor of. The best result is often someone in operations or finance who can simply tell you who owns sales training decisions.

Run this the day any account enters Tier 1. It takes ninety seconds and it changes how you approach the account.
  • Past company: Lushin. Add other Sandler affiliates if useful.
  • Current company: Lushin, with exclude switched on — otherwise you get everyone who was promoted or changed roles internally.
  • Then narrow by your geography and buyer titles.

Why it works: they already know the methodology and what it costs. There is no education step. And someone internally almost certainly knows them well enough to make the introduction.

This is the one Sam calls magic, and it is the highest-yield search on the list for you specifically, because Lushin's client base is concentrated in a few Indiana industries where people move between firms constantly.

  • Past company: your best clients — Browning Day, Keramida, Hubexo, Home Nation, Next Modular, Bulwark, PartnerCentric.
  • Current company: your A1 and A2 account lists, dropped in with one click.
  • Title: your buyer titles. Geography: your footprint.

The opener writes itself: they were at a firm that hired Lushin. They either went through the training or watched it happen. You are not cold.

  • Connections of: one colleague at a time. You must be 1st degree with them, which you are.
  • Current company: your A1 account list.
  • Title: your buyer titles.

The problem with this search: it returns people your colleague is technically connected to but does not actually know. Narrow it by adding the things that make a real relationship likely — their previous employers, their school, their city. Two people who worked at the same firm in the same city probably know each other.

This is your substitute for TeamLink, and honestly it is more precise, because you are choosing which colleagues to mine rather than getting every opted-in employee.

  • Past company: your own previous employers.
  • Current company: your account lists, or leave open and filter by geography.
  • Connection: try 2nd degree specifically — the 1st degrees you already know about.

The 2nd-degree filter is the trick. It surfaces people from your own history that you never met, but who share connections with you who can vouch.

1 Saved searches are the passive engine

A saved search does not just store filters. It watches LinkedIn and tells you when a new person starts matching. That is the difference between prospecting and being fed, and it is the only automation in this whole system that carries no risk.

  • What still needs rationing is alert volume. Fifty searches all pinging you is fifty searches you stop reading. Keep the frequent alerts to two or three.

2 The exclusions base

This is the one genuinely useful Sales Navigator technique in the LCL material, and it is worth the twenty minutes it takes. The idea: your buyer titles pull in a lot of adjacent noise, and rather than re-filtering it every search, you build the exclusions once and save them as a reusable base.

Run a deliberately broad search

Your buyer titles, your geography, nothing else. Look at a result count in the thousands.

Scroll and harvest the noise

Every off-target title and keyword you see, write it down. For your market that means recruiters, staffing, insurance and financial advisors, real estate agents, marketing agencies, coaches selling to coaches, and anyone whose headline is a course.

Exclude them one at a time, watching the count

In the keyword field use NOT before each term, and quote multi-word terms: NOT recruiter NOT staffing NOT "financial advisor" NOT realtor. In the title field, exclude titles directly.

Stop when the count drops too far

If one exclusion takes out 600 results, it probably caught real prospects too. Back it out and be more specific.

Save it as "BASE — default exclusions"

You must press Enter in the keyword field before saving or the keyword won't stick.

Open the base every time you start a new search

Load it, then change the company, title, or geography on top. Your exclusion list survives, and it grows every time you notice new noise.

Paste into the keyword field
NOT recruiter NOT recruiting NOT staffing NOT "talent acquisition" NOT realtor NOT "real estate agent" NOT "financial advisor" NOT insurance NOT "life coach" NOT "business coach" NOT franchise NOT MLM

Exclude business coach and life coach in the title field rather than keywords if it cuts too deep — some legitimate prospects mention coaching in their About section.

3 Alert frequency

Two searches get the frequent alert; everything else gets the default. More than that and you stop reading them, which is worse than not having them at all. The two that earn it are the ones where timing is the entire value: a client alumnus landing somewhere new, and a new sales leader taking a seat.

Move, never copy. If they appear in two stage lists you have lost track of what you have already said to them, which is how you end up sending someone a first-touch message four months into a conversation.

Every action gets a dated note

In the lead's note field, one line per action: DF commented 09/09, DF CR sent 09/14, DF asked 09/28. This is the whole memory of the system. Skip it and in six weeks you are guessing.

Cap every list

Vetting at 100, Top 50 at about 50. A list you cannot work through in its cadence is not a list, it's a graveyard. When one overflows, split it by industry.

Nothing sits in a stage list without a next date

If you cannot say when you will next touch this person, they belong in Parked.

Delete lists you built for one purpose

Building a throwaway list to scan one account's leadership team is a good move. Delete it when you are done. Sam does exactly this and it keeps the sidebar honest.

2 How to ask for the introduction

1 The idea

Rather than appearing in someone's inbox from nowhere, you appear in their notifications two or three times first, adding something to posts they wrote. By the time your connection request lands, your name is familiar. Sam calls this expanding the sandbox; it is the highest-conversion cold play in either course and it is slow on purpose.

Find who is posting

Search 9 — one Tier 1 account, your buyer titles, Posted on LinkedIn ON.

Open their profile on LinkedIn.com, not in Navigator

Navigator's activity data lags. Use the three-dot menu to open the real profile in a new tab so you keep your search results intact. Then check the Posts tab specifically.

Qualify their activity

Three tests: do they post at least two or three times a month; is it their own writing rather than reshares; and is it about something broader than their own company. Fail any of the three and close the tab — commenting on a reshare puts you in front of the original author, not your prospect.

Comment with something that adds to the post

Not a like. Not agreement. Add a perspective, extend the point, or bring in a specific example. The bar: would the author be glad you commented?

Save them to L1 — Vetting and note the date

DF commented 09/09. Leave space between comments — a few days at least — and comment on one previous post at most.

At two or three comments, move to L2 and send the request

Now the note writes itself, because you have actually been reading their work.

2 What a comment that works looks like

Adds something

  • Agree, then add your own angle from what you have seen across clients.
  • Extend the point one step further than they took it.
  • Bring a specific example — a firm, a number, a situation.
  • Tag someone genuinely relevant and say why the post would matter to them. This is the strongest move available and it shows what circles you run in.

Adds nothing

  • "Great post!" or an emoji.
  • Restating what they said in different words.
  • Disagreeing to be noticed. This is not the moment.
  • Anything that pivots to what you sell.

The posts do not have to be about work. If a firm owner posts about coaching their kid's team, comment on that. They cared enough to write it.

3 Where the volume goes

You are not doing this to a hundred people. Six or seven people per Tier 1 account, capped at about twenty active at once, so you are making an impression across several firms rather than going deep at one.

Key

The reason this works is that almost nobody does it. Every one of your competitors is sending the connection request first.

OpenLinkedIn messages

1 The five things a DM has to convey

Before any script, the model underneath it. Every message is judged on five things at once, and most sales DMs fail three of them in the first line.

Relevant

You clearly understand their world. Wrong industry or wrong title and nothing else matters.

Competent

You know something specific about their space that only someone who works in it would know.

Socially aware

You read the room, match their energy, and don't push when they're not pushing.

Genuinely interested

You are asking because you want to know, not because it's step two of a sequence.

Not needy

Your time is not infinitely available and you are not desperate for this meeting. This is the one everyone gets wrong.

Not automated

Nothing about the message reads as a template. Everyone has an automation radar now.

2 How the message is built

Open with something restrained and specific

Not "I absolutely love your work." Dial it down: "Solid concept behind what you're building at [firm]." Over-enthusiasm reads as someone who wants something. Understated reads as a peer.

Run two threads at once

One business, one human. Their firm and their city, their offer and the thing on their profile. The human thread fades naturally after a few exchanges; the business thread carries on.

State their offer back to them, don't ask it

"You're doing design-build for healthcare clients" beats "Do you do design-build?" One says you did the research; the other says you didn't.

Stack two or three questions, never one

A single question reads like an interview. Two or three, with an observation attached, reads like a conversation and gives them something to pick up.

End on the human thread, not the ask

The last line of your message is what shows in their inbox preview. If it's "how are you growing right now?" they know they're being sold and won't open it.

3 Format rules

  • Never one block of text. Send three or four short separate messages rather than one paragraph. A block reads as automated; separate bubbles read as someone typing.
  • Vary line length deliberately. Short, long, short. It looks off the cuff because it is how people actually type.
  • Say it out loud first. If you would not say the sentence to someone across a table, don't send it. This one test fixes most bad DMs.
  • Never edit or delete a sent message. LinkedIn shows the edit marker and it reads as caring too much. Correct it in the next message.
  • Drop the final question mark. Small thing, but it reads as relaxed rather than demanding.

4 The mindset that drives all of it

The frame both courses land on, from opposite directions, is the same: you are trying to help, and whether that turns into business is genuinely a secondary question. Sam calls it earning the right to the meeting. LCL calls it the friendly helper. It is not a technique — if you are performing it, it shows. But it is the reason the messages land, because everything else in their inbox is transparently trying to get something.

Key

This should be the easiest part of the system for you. It is what you already do in a first meeting. The DM is that same posture, in writing, before the meeting exists.

Message 1
Hey Mike - solid work at Shiel Sexton. Saw the Ball State project write-up.
Message 2 (a beat later)
You're running the whole preconstruction and BD side there, or is that split
Message 3
Curious how you're handling BD with the PMs. Most of the AEC firms I work with around Indy have the same problem - the people with the relationships are billable, so business development happens whenever there's a gap, which is never
Message 4
Fellow Indy guy by the way. Been out at the Ball State campus a few times, it's come a long way

Four short messages. One restrained compliment, one stated observation about their role, one authority line that only someone who works in their world would say, and a human close. No ask anywhere.

3 Moves

  • Restrained compliments. Enthusiasm at a four or five out of ten, never an eight. Never words with a negative edge either — not decent, not not bad for a small firm.
  • Authority observations. Say something about their world that only someone inside it could say. "Most AEC firms I work with have 60% of revenue coming from four clients and no one wants to say it out loud." This builds more trust than any case study, because it is not about you.
  • Name-drop with context, in their niche only. Not "we work with lots of firms." Rather: "Chuck at Bulwark and I have been at this two years — he's the one who sent me your way." A name without a relationship attached does nothing.
  • Mirror and add one. If they're brief, be brief. If they open up, open up slightly more. Matching a one-word reply with enthusiasm reads as not listening.
  • Spontaneity markers. "Actually, just thought of something." "By the way." "Sitting here looking at your site right now." These signal a person at a keyboard rather than a scheduled send.
  • Foreshadow value before you ask anything. Mention who you could introduce them to, or something you could send, before you start asking about their business. It changes what the questions feel like.

4 Two moves worth knowing but not copying

  • The deliberate typo — misspelling an easy word and correcting it in the next message, to prove a human sent it. It works, but it is beneath the positioning you already have locally. Skip it.
  • Foreshadowing an introduction you don't intend to make — LCL is explicit that the intro only gets made if they ask for it, which is most of the time never. Don't do this. Offer introductions you will actually make. You live in a small market and a broken promise costs more than the meeting is worth.
  • What does transfer: genuinely offering to connect someone to a person who would help them. You are unusually well-positioned for this — you know a lot of Indiana business owners and most of them would take a call from you.

Asking too early is the most common failure and the most expensive one. You do not get a second first impression, and a premature ask converts the whole thread into a sales sequence in their mind.

What makes you different, in one clause

Not a paragraph. One thing.

The ask, unsure of your own availability

Then you send the invite. Do not send a booking link into a cold thread with a firm owner; it reads as processing them.

3 After they say yes

  • Ask them to confirm once it's booked. One line. It closes the loop and gives you a reason to message again.
  • Send something useful immediately after booking — a short piece of value related to what you discussed, sent as its own message. It reframes the meeting from a sales call to a continuation.
  • Get a phone number in the booking flow and text them the morning of. Both courses claim a meaningful lift in show rate and it costs nothing.
  • Keep the calendar looking used. Thirty-minute slots rather than fifteen, a booking window five or six days out rather than a month. A calendar with forty open slots tells them nobody wants your time.

4 When the answer is no

  • Reply anyway. A short thank-you costs nothing and leaves the door open. Most people go silent when they hear no, which is the worst possible ending.
  • "Not interested" is not the same as "never." They read it, they replied, they are active. Note the date and park them.
  • "Call me in 90 days" is an invitation to nurture, not to disappear for 90 days. Send them something useful once a month with no ask attached.
  • Don't ask why they aren't interested. You have not earned that answer yet and asking for it reads as pushing past a boundary. After a meeting that didn't close, asking why is entirely fair.

Anyone who has been through the full sequence with no reply goes to L4 — Parked, revisited at 90 to 120 days. Two things make this list worth keeping rather than deleting:

  • You get their job changes for free. A parked prospect who moves firms is a brand new conversation with none of the history.
  • Timing changes everything and you cannot see it. The reason they ignored you in March may have resolved itself by September. Nothing you did was wrong.

1 The order matters more than the duration

Work warmest first, always. The instinct is to start new conversations because they feel like progress; the discipline is to finish the warm ones sitting in front of you. Someone who replied yesterday is worth more than fifty people who have never heard of you.

Alerts — job changes and new decision makers

Two minutes. Anything in these two categories jumps the queue and gets acted on today. A sales leader who started this week will not still be looking in six weeks.

Inbound replies

Anyone who answered you. All of them, before you do anything else.

These expire. Someone on your target list looked at you; the window where that is a natural reason to reach out is days, not weeks.

Whoever accepted since yesterday. There is no urgency to this — both courses agree the difference between messaging within a day and within a few weeks is small — but it is easy to let them pile up.

Anyone in L3 whose gap has elapsed.

Last, because it is the coldest and the easiest to do on autopilot. Eight to ten a day, from the searches.

1 One list, ninety minutes

Monday is your Top 50 block, and it is the only structured prospecting hour in the week. Monday works for this because it is the worst day to post — everyone posts Monday morning and competes for the same attention — so you spend it reading and engaging rather than publishing.

Open S1 — Top 50 and read the header row first

How many changed jobs, how many posted in the last 30 days, how many follow Lushin. Those numbers set the agenda before you look at a single name.

Work the job changes first

Every one. Where did they go, and who filled the seat behind them? That is two conversations from one alert.

Take the next 10 to 12 names in rotation

Not the same names every week. Work down the list in order, so across four or five weeks everyone gets touched, then start again.

For each: open their profile on LinkedIn.com and read their activity

What have they posted? What have they commented on? Comment on something — and prefer commenting on a post they commented on, because that notifies them, whereas both of you liking the same thing does not.

Update the note with the date

DF commented 09/09. Without this the rotation falls apart within a month.

Anyone who's gone quiet for two full rotations

Ask whether they belong in Top 50 at all. This list should stay at roughly 50 — every addition ought to displace someone.

Key

Ten to twelve people a week, on a fifty-person list, means every person on it hears from you roughly every five weeks, forever, without you having to remember anyone.

3 The Friday re-read

The most-emphasized habit in the LCL material and the one everybody skips. Pick two conversations from the week — ideally one that booked and one that died — and read them back cold. You will see things you could not see while writing them. Distance is the whole mechanism; a message that felt sharp on Tuesday reads clumsy on Friday, and that gap is where the skill actually improves.

  • Pick one specific thing to work on the following week. Not "be better at DMs" — something like "stop asking a single question," or "open with an authority observation every time."
  • Run that one thing deliberately across every message for a week. Then pick the next thing.
  • Two or three of these a month compounds faster than reading more material.

1 Monthly — about an hour

  • ✓Job changes across every lead list. Filter each list by career changes. Every move is two conversations - the person's new seat, and the hole they left.
  • ✓L4 — Parked, anyone past 90 days. Re-approach with something new. Not a bump - a reason.

  • ✓Check who follows the Lushin page across your lead lists. Anyone new is worth a look at why.
  • ✓Clear the saved-alerts queue. The things you flagged during the month and never got to.
  • ✓Withdraw connection requests older than about a month. Keeps the pending count clean.

2 Quarterly — about two hours

  • Re-tier every account. Anything in Tier 1 you have not touched in a quarter goes to Tier 2. This is the discipline that keeps Tier 1 meaning something.
  • Prune saved-search alerts, not the searches themselves — you have plenty of slots. Any alert you have stopped reading gets switched off.
  • Update the exclusions base with the noise you have noticed since last time.
  • Review the scorecard and check your actual conversion rates against the assumptions in your quota math. Adjust the activity numbers, not the goal.
  • Refresh the closed-deal seed list if any engagements have closed or ended.

3 Annually

  • Rebuild the alumni searches from scratch. New clients means new past-company seeds, which means the highest-yield search gets better every year you run this.
  • Re-do the quota math against what actually happened rather than what you assumed.
  • Profile pass. New proof, new numbers, new featured items.
Key

This system gets stronger with age, which is unusual. Every closed deal adds a seed to the alumni searches. Every year of saved leads adds job changes. The person who has run this for three years has an asset the person starting today cannot buy.

1 The honest position

Both courses touch automation and both are more cautious than the tools that sponsor them. LCL recommends a connection-request automation tool and then says three separate times not to make it the main strategy — his own framing is that it is five to ten percent of what they do. Sam does not use it at all.

Key

For your deal size, automated LinkedIn outreach is a bad trade. Not because it is unethical, but because the thing being automated - the personalization - is the entire reason the message works.

2 Where automation is fine

TaskVerdict
Calendar booking, questionnaire, remindersYes. Highest-value automation available and nobody perceives it as automation. Capture a phone number in the booking form.
Pre-meeting email sequenceYes. Two or three messages before a booked meeting. Improves show rate and sets the frame.
CRM stage tracking and task remindersYes. Where your follow-up dates should actually live, rather than in Navigator notes alone.
Sales Navigator alerts and saved searchesYes — and this is the automation that matters. It automates finding, which is the expensive part, and leaves the human part human.
Drafting posts and comments with AICareful. Fine for idea generation and first drafts. Not fine for sending unedited — there is now a strong detection instinct for AI-written comments, and being caught costs more than the comment was worth.
Automated connection requestsNo, at your volume. You are sending 20–40 a week from targeted searches. That is ten minutes of manual work. There is nothing to save.
Automated DMsNo. This is the one that damages you. Everyone has a template radar now, and an owner who spots a template from a sales trainer will draw exactly the conclusion you would rather they didn't.
Auto-commentingNo. Explicitly warned against in the LCL material, and it is the fastest way to look like a bot to the exact people you are trying to impress.
Auto-liking and auto-endorsingNo. Trivial upside, real account risk.

3 Account risk

  • Third-party automation violates LinkedIn's user agreement, whatever the vendor's marketing says. Enforcement is inconsistent, but restriction and permanent ban are both real outcomes.
  • Your LinkedIn account is a professional asset with a decade of relationships in it. Weigh a possible ban against ten minutes a day saved.
  • The advice to ignore LinkedIn's warnings until hard-blocked that appears in the LCL material is not advice you should follow.

4 What to actually automate

The most valuable automation in this whole system is already built into what you are paying for. Saved searches with alerts are an automation: they watch LinkedIn continuously and hand you qualified, well-timed names without you doing anything. Nine saved searches running daily and weekly will produce more usable leads than any outreach tool, and they carry zero account risk.
